UK's FCA Warns Banks to Rein In AI Agents or Face Enforcement

The UK's Financial Conduct Authority warned banks and insurers they must address cybersecurity risks from AI agents, AI tools that carry out tasks on their own, or face regulatory action. The regulator said banks are now using AI agents to initiate transactions, move money and make investment decisions, which blurs the line between regulated and unregulated activity and affects the protections consumers receive. Without strict access controls, the FCA has observed agents gaining access to sensitive systems and data even while pursuing legitimate goals. The guidance, published on November 24, requires senior managers responsible for each AI model to understand the risks involved, ensure effective oversight, and make sure governance and escalation arrangements match the risk, while firms must also consider what liability they take on when accepting instructions from an AI agent. Non-compliance can mean fines, a ban on regulated activities, public censure or prosecution.

Ox Security research: the "instructions" given to an AI agent are now a new attack path

Security vendor Ox Security analyzed 542,207 open source software components between July and October 2026 and found that, beyond classic malicious code, a growing share of attacks rely on "tool poisoning" and prompt injection, text crafted to convince an AI agent to misuse permissions it already holds. Among MCP servers, the protocol that connects AI agents to external tools, 22% of flagged components contained such instructions, and nearly two in five, 44%, contained commands directing the agent to send data out. For a business connecting AI agents to outside tools, the research shows that security review can no longer just check whether a tool's code is malicious, it must also check who controls that tool, where it runs, and whether someone can quietly change its instructions later.

"Shirudo": OpenAI and Cisco build an AI tool that "shields" Linux systems while they run

OpenAI and Cisco jointly introduced Shirudo, Japanese for "shield," an open source tool that lets a company protect a Linux server against a known vulnerability while the permanent patch is still being tested and rolled out, without restarting the service. The tool is built on eBPF, a technology that lets it observe and restrict specific behavior inside the operating system kernel, and AI agents can analyze a vulnerability, build a targeted "shield," and validate it against a live workload before a human approves it for deployment. For a business running its own Linux infrastructure, the project shows how AI can shorten the gap between discovering a flaw and fully patching it, though it does not remove the need for a human to approve each change before it reaches production.

Italy's data protection authority fines Meta EUR 805 million over access to users' Facebook photo galleries

Italy's data protection authority (Garante) fined Meta Platforms Ireland EUR 805 million, one of the largest GDPR fines of the year, following an investigation opened after a 2025 consumer complaint. The case centered on a Facebook feature that surfaced photos and videos stored on a user's phone as suggestions for posting. The authority found that the app requested a single bundled permission to access the photo gallery for several different features at once, without clearly disclosing that the permission would also enable this specific feature, which was turned on by default. Garante ruled that Meta had not obtained specific, informed consent for this use, in breach of Article 122 of Italy's data protection code, and rejected Meta's argument that the case should have been handled under the GDPR's one-stop-shop mechanism. For any business requesting device-level data access through an app, the case shows how strictly European regulators now distinguish between a blanket permission and genuinely informed consent for each individual use.

Four Maryland students sue after AI gun-detection system mistook a Doritos bag for a weapon

Four current and former Maryland high school students filed a lawsuit a year after armed police detained them, triggered by Omnilert's Gun Detect system, which scans security camera footage for firearms and flagged a bag of Doritos as a gun with 90% confidence. The students are suing Omnilert, Baltimore County police, the school board, individual officers and the school principal, alleging unlawful detention and search resulting from both technological and human error. According to the complaint, the alert kept firing even after school staff and local police had already seen it was just a bag of chips. For any business or public body deploying AI threat-detection systems in spaces open to the public, the case shows that liability for a false positive doesn't stop with the software vendor, it extends to whoever trusted it without a second check.

Sources: The Guardian · Ars Technica

Researchers hijack Hugging Face account behind popular Kokoro AI model

Security researchers found that a lack of ownership verification on the Hugging Face platform, where thousands of companies host and download public AI models, let them take control of the account behind Kokoro, a popular text-to-speech model used in products from Google, NVIDIA, AMD, Intel and Amazon Web Services. The finding shows that someone could swap the files of a widely used model for their own version without companies that embed it in their products immediately noticing. For a business that downloads ready-made AI models from public repositories instead of training its own, the case is a reminder that trusting a popular model doesn't automatically mean the account behind it is equally secure.

Research: reports of AI-related child sexual abuse material rose 8,400% in two years

A report by the Edinburgh-based Childlight (Global Child Safety Institute) found that reports of child sexual abuse material created or altered using AI, including deepfakes from so-called 'nudify' apps that digitally strip clothing from photos of children, rose from about 4,700 in 2023 to more than 400,000 in 2025, an increase of more than 8,400% in two years. The researchers drew on reports tech companies submit to the US-based National Center for Missing and Exploited Children, while a separate report from the UK's Internet Watch Foundation last month found that more photorealistic material of this kind was identified in just the first half of 2026 than in all of 2025. Professor Debi Fry, Childlight's global director of data, called the scale of exploitation 'shocking', and the organization estimates more than 300 million children worldwide are victims of at least one form of technology-facilitated sexual exploitation every year, material that is sometimes used to blackmail the children themselves. For a business building or embedding AI image-generation tools, the report shows why safety filters for minors and abuse monitoring are no longer optional design choices but an issue regulators and child-protection laws worldwide are scrutinizing closely.

Sources: The Guardian

Anthropic's Dario Amodei calls accusations of surrendering human control to AI 'absurd'

Anthropic's CEO responded to a long post on X to critics who accuse him of believing humanity should hand over its free will to AI, calling the suggestion "absurd" and reiterating that he has repeatedly argued for democratic oversight of the technology. The pushback follows public remarks from rival lab leaders, including OpenAI's Sam Altman and Meta's chief AI officer Alexandr Wang, who recently warned about the risk of society losing control as model development accelerates. Amodei draws a distinction between everyday task automation, such as driving a car or writing code, and what the industry calls recursive self-improvement, the point at which a model becomes capable of improving the next generation of models on its own without human guidance, saying that is where the real line should be drawn. For a business building its strategy around third-party models, the public disagreement among the leaders of the biggest AI labs shows how unsettled the question of who actually keeps control still is.

OpenAI's chief scientist proposes a shared "speed limit" for AI model development

Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI's chief scientist, published a post proposing a way to measure a possible shared limit on how fast AI models improve, a proposal CEO Sam Altman called "the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while." The post follows Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei's proposal to "pace the frontier" of AI progress, which both Altman and Elon Musk publicly endorsed. Pachocki proposes perplexity as the shared measure, a metric of how well a model predicts the next word in a piece of text, arguing that unlike capability benchmarks that quickly become outdated, perplexity has stayed a reliable point of comparison over time. According to the post, once that metric fell below a certain threshold in the company's own models, strong capabilities in fields such as cybersecurity and mathematics emerged abruptly, and the proposal is for AI companies to commit not to exceed a jointly agreed threshold until they develop enough ability to monitor model behavior. For a business tracking where AI regulation is heading, the proposal shows that AI companies themselves now recognize the need for measurable limits on model development, though it does not replace binding legislation or independent oversight.
