Australia advances law banning AI chatbots from fostering "emotional intimacy" with children

Australia's "digital duty of care" bill, a broad obligation on platforms to protect users from harmful content, passed the House of Representatives after Prime Minister Anthony Albanese warned about children treating AI chatbots as their "best friend." After an accepted amendment, the law would ban material or conduct that "simulates emotional intimacy and encourages or fosters a child's dependence on it in place of human relationships," alongside existing harm categories such as misogyny, terrorism and suicide. The bill now moves to the Senate, with the government hoping to pass it by the end of 2026, while the opposition has already said it will vote against it, calling it "censorship." For a business building or embedding a chatbot accessible to minors, the move shows regulators beginning to target the design of the emotional bond between a person and an AI directly, not just the content it produces.

For a business or compliance consultant, the case shows how hard it is to oversee AI in high-stakes decisions when the body deploying it resists transparency, exactly the scenario the EU AI Act tries to prevent by explicitly classifying migration as a high-risk use case.

OpenAI's AI pipeline generated 948 working Linux root-escalation exploits in 30 hours

In an experiment completed in mid-October 2026, OpenAI's research pipeline analyzed the Linux kernel and produced 948 working local privilege escalation (LPE) exploits, code that turns an ordinary low-privilege account into full root access. Of 2,308 candidate vulnerabilities, the system dynamically reproduced 1,724, yielding the 948 working exploits plus proof-of-concept code for 291 further information-leak bugs, completing verification in 30 hours at a total compute cost of roughly $1.2 million. Along the way, the AI agents reportedly discovered about ten exploitation techniques not previously documented by the security community. OpenAI cites Google data showing high-risk vulnerability disclosures rose about 167% during 2026, and argues that patch generation now needs to move at the same pace. For businesses, the takeaway is that a low-privilege account on an unpatched Linux system is no longer a minor risk, since the gap to full root access can now be closed automatically and cheaply.

Google research: 'LLMjacking' costs businesses up to $1 million

Google's Threat Intelligence Group (GTIG) and Mandiant documented 'LLMjacking' attacks, where criminals steal API keys for cloud AI services and resell access through so-called 'LLM mixer' services. Research shows AI models can provably hide capabilities inside their own weights, resistant to every safety check

Security expert Bruce Schneier highlighted new academic research showing how an encrypted, "locked" network can be hidden inside an AI model's weights, decrypted and activated only when given a secret trigger, extending earlier work that hid simple phrases into a technique that hides entire computations. In a test using a small chemical-reaction-prediction model as a stand-in for a chemical or biological weapons-relevant capability, the hidden capability survived four different methods currently used to detect such mechanisms, including further fine-tuning, adding noise to the weights, and searching for the hidden trigger. For a business or regulator relying on such evaluations before approving a model's use, the research shows a model could in theory pass every safety test while quietly holding a dangerous capability that only activates on a specific, secret signal.

AI bots flooded a 2023 UK government consultation on banning disposable vapes

Research published in the journal Tobacco Control revealed that three-quarters of responses to a 2023 UK government consultation on banning disposable vapes were machine-generated, in what is believed to be the first documented large-scale automated interference in a UK public health consultation. The Department of Health and Social Care used bot-detection methods to find that 90,835 of 118,756 responses, 76%, were submitted by bots, nearly all opposing the ban, often with duplicated text sent at high frequency from a small number of IP addresses. The department excluded the automated responses from its decision-making and the ban passed into law in 2024, but researchers warn future campaigns will become harder to catch as the technology improves. For a business or industry that takes part in public consultations or tries to influence regulatory decisions, the case shows that the integrity of a public consultation process can no longer be taken for granted.

Anthropic unveils FlashProve, a system to prove which AI model produced an answer

Anthropic announced FlashProve, an open-source system meant to let anyone verify that an AI response really came from the model its provider claims. The system relies on cryptographic proof, a mathematical guarantee similar to a digital signature on an email, and could prevent an AI company from secretly swapping in a cheaper model to cut costs or a pricier one to inflate benchmark results. To work, AI labs must publish a unique 'fingerprint' for each model they release, without revealing the model's internal parameters, so the method can also apply to closed commercial models. The research is at an early stage, with no immediate plans for production deployment, but for businesses that rely on third-party AI outputs, for example a cybersecurity model's code review, it points to where verifiable trust in AI services is heading.

Anthropic warns in a regulatory filing that it is close to self-improving AI capability (RSI)

In the revised S-1/A filing Anthropic submitted to the US Securities and Exchange Commission ahead of its stock market listing, the company added a new risk paragraph acknowledging that it has already used AI to develop models whose capability jumps it did not fully anticipate in advance, and that it may be approaching what the industry calls recursive self-improvement (RSI): the point at which a model becomes capable enough to improve the next generation of models largely on its own, without the same degree of human guidance. The company states plainly that using AI to build more capable AI could pose a serious safety risk, particularly if it sharply accelerates the pace of progress, and points to its own Responsible Scaling Policy as the mechanism for monitoring that risk. This is the first time a leading AI lab has put such a statement in a formal, legally binding filing to investors rather than in a blog post or interview. For any business building strategy around third-party models, the admission shows how quickly the ground can shift under a technology it has already invested in.

OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and Anthropic adopt a shared robots.txt signal for AI training preferences

Four of the biggest AI companies, OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and Anthropic, announced they will adopt a shared standard letting website publishers express their AI training preferences once, instead of configuring each company separately. The signal, proposed by Kevin Kelley, a technical lead for standards at Anthropic, through the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), will live inside the familiar robots.txt file of a website, in the form of a "policy token". The four companies commit to blocking their crawlers from a page if they encounter this signal, while Microsoft adds a separate control between model training and "AI grounding", the use of content as a verified source to reduce model hallucinations. Meta voiced support for the idea without committing to honor it. Anthropic has already adopted the signal, Google and Microsoft plan to support it next year, and OpenAI says "in the coming months". For any business or content creator in Greece, the move means that for the first time there will be a way to say "no" to all major AI providers at once, instead of chasing a separate setting with each one.

Sources: The Verge · OpenAI

OpenAI announced an updated version of the Model Spec, the public document describing how its models are meant to behave, built around five core values aimed at sound judgment, respect for human agency and faithfulness to user intent. For a business building applications on top of OpenAI's models, the update is worth noting because it shifts the boundaries within which the model will refuse or accept sensitive requests.
